AETL OPINION PAPER NO. 002

Before the Agenda: Why Every Newly Transferred Minister Must First Learn to Listen

By The Rev’d. Olumayowa Alaba Akinbade

AETL Opinion Series • Vol. 1, No. 2

Published: 7 September 2026

Introduction

This reflection does not arise from decades of serving as a senior minister. It emerges from my theological reflection, my service as a minister-in-charge, an assistant minister and Archbishop’s Chaplain in the Methodist Church Nigeria, as well as my observation of ministers whose ministries have flourished or struggled during periods of pastoral transition. These experiences have persuaded me that one of the first responsibilities of a newly transferred minister is not simply to lead but to listen.

A pastoral transfer often comes with expectations. The congregation anticipates direction, the minister hopes to make a meaningful contribution, and the wider Church expects the ministry to advance. Sometimes, a new minister arrives with ideas about what needs to change. These ideas may be sincere, practical and even necessary. Yet good intentions do not automatically produce good leadership. A change that appears obvious to the incoming minister may be experienced differently by a congregation whose history, struggles and relationships are not yet fully understood.

The question, therefore, is not whether a newly transferred minister should lead. Leadership is part of the calling. The more important question is whether the minister is prepared to understand the people and circumstances entrusted to their care before deciding how best to lead them.

Before the agenda comes listening. Before major decisions come understanding. And before the desire to make an impact comes the responsibility to discern what faithful ministry requires in that particular place.

A Biblical Theology of Listening and Leadership

The biblical witness presents listening as an essential part of faithful leadership. In Exodus 3:7, God tells Moses that he has seen the suffering of the Israelites, heard their cry and understood their pain. God’s response to suffering begins with attentive knowledge of the people’s condition.

Although divine listening cannot simply be equated with human leadership, the passage offers an important principle: meaningful intervention requires an awareness of the reality being addressed. A leader who acts without understanding may respond to the situation as imagined rather than as it actually exists.

The ministry of Jesus also demonstrates the importance of attending to people. He encountered individuals in their particular circumstances, asked questions, received their responses and addressed needs that were not always immediately apparent to others. His engagement with people was neither mechanical nor indifferent to their lived experiences.

Listening, however, did not make Jesus captive to public opinion. He sometimes challenged the expectations of the crowd and confronted conduct that contradicted God’s purposes. His example shows that attentive leadership is not the same as people-pleasing. Listening helps a leader understand people; discernment helps the leader respond faithfully.

For a newly transferred minister, this distinction matters. The congregation should be heard, but its preferences cannot automatically determine every pastoral decision. Scripture, the Church’s mission, sound theological judgment and the realities of the local ministry must all inform the way forward.

Listening as a Spiritual Discipline

Listening is more than a communication technique. For Christian ministers, it is a spiritual discipline that requires humility, patience and a willingness to learn.

A minister may arrive with theological training, pastoral experience and a genuine desire to strengthen the Church. These are valuable resources. Yet no qualification or previous experience can eliminate the need to understand a new congregation. The minister must still learn its history, relationships, patterns of worship, sources of strength and areas of concern.

Some congregations have endured painful leadership transitions. Others have developed effective practices through years of faithful service. Some may be struggling with unresolved conflicts, declining participation or financial difficulties. These realities cannot be understood adequately through a few formal meetings or reports alone.

Listening requires presence. It involves conversations with church officers, older members, younger people, women, men, lay leaders and those whose voices may not ordinarily be heard. It also means observing how people relate to one another, how decisions are made and how the congregation responds to challenges.

Above all, the minister must listen prayerfully. Not every complaint represents an injustice, just as not every established practice deserves to continue. Prayer, Scripture, reflection and honest conversation help the minister distinguish between what requires correction, what deserves preservation and what needs time to mature.

A minister who listens well is not demonstrating weakness. The minister is acknowledging that pastoral responsibility begins with a commitment to understand before attempting to direct.

The Danger of Arriving with a Ready-Made Agenda

One danger in pastoral transition is the assumption that the new minister must quickly demonstrate effectiveness by introducing visible changes.

This pressure may arise from personal ambition, institutional expectations or a genuine desire to improve the congregation. A minister may believe that a new programme, administrative arrangement or style of worship will bring immediate progress. Yet the desire to make an early impact can lead to decisions made before the underlying situation is understood.

The problem is not that change is unnecessary. Churches must respond to changing circumstances, correct harmful practices and pursue new opportunities for ministry. The problem arises when change becomes a demonstration of authority rather than the outcome of careful discernment.

A congregation is not a blank canvas awaiting the arrival of a new minister. It has a history, a spiritual life, established relationships and people who have contributed to its growth. Even practices that appear inefficient may have developed in response to circumstances the incoming minister has not yet encountered.

When a minister dismisses existing arrangements too quickly, members may interpret the action as a rejection of their contributions. Resistance may follow, not necessarily because the congregation opposes progress, but because its members feel that their experience has been disregarded.

The minister may then mistake resistance for disloyalty, while the congregation interprets leadership as insensitivity. What begins as an attempt to improve ministry can become a struggle over authority and recognition.

The lesson is not to avoid change but to ensure that change is informed by understanding. A new minister does not have to prove leadership by changing everything. Sometimes, the first evidence of good leadership is the wisdom to know what not to change.

The Other Extreme: Leadership by Popular Opinion

If premature change is one danger, excessive dependence on popular opinion is another.

Listening can be misunderstood as the obligation to satisfy everyone. A minister may become so concerned about maintaining acceptance that difficult decisions are postponed, necessary correction is avoided and influential members are allowed to determine the direction of the congregation.

This is not responsible pastoral leadership. The Church is not merely a community of competing preferences, and the minister’s calling cannot be reduced to keeping everyone comfortable.

There are situations in which faithful leadership requires difficult decisions. Misconduct may need to be addressed, unhealthy practices may have to end, and resources may need to be redirected towards more pressing priorities. Such decisions can produce dissatisfaction even when they are necessary.

The responsibility of a minister is therefore not to secure universal approval but to act with integrity, fairness and spiritual discernment. Where change is necessary, the minister should communicate its reasons clearly, listen to legitimate concerns and follow appropriate ecclesiastical procedures. Where correction is required, it should be undertaken without humiliation or personal hostility.

Listening should inform leadership, not replace it. The minister must be open enough to learn from the congregation and courageous enough to act when conscience, Scripture and responsible pastoral judgment demand action.

The Ministry of Discernment

The period following a transfer should be understood as an opportunity for discernment. It is a time to learn what the congregation needs, identify its strengths and weaknesses, and consider how its life and witness can be strengthened.

Discernment involves asking questions before reaching conclusions. What has sustained this congregation through difficult seasons? Which ministries are bearing fruit? Where are members experiencing discouragement? Are there unresolved relationships affecting the Church’s work? Which concerns require immediate attention, and which would benefit from patient engagement?

These questions help the minister move beyond assumptions. They also prevent the temptation to interpret every difference from previous pastoral experience as evidence that something is wrong.

Discernment does not require an indefinite period of inaction. Certain matters, particularly those involving safety, misconduct, serious financial irregularities or other urgent risks, may demand prompt attention. Listening must not become an excuse for neglecting immediate responsibilities.

The point is to distinguish between urgent matters that require timely intervention and longer-term questions that deserve fuller understanding. A wise minister knows that not every issue should be handled at the same speed or through the same process.

The goal is not simply to gather information. It is to understand the congregation well enough to make decisions that are timely, proportionate and consistent with the Church’s mission.

The Shepherd Must Know the Sheep

The biblical image of the shepherd offers a useful way to understand pastoral leadership. A shepherd does not care for an abstract flock but for living creatures with particular needs and vulnerabilities. The image points towards attentiveness, responsibility and care.

For a minister, knowing the congregation means more than learning names or remembering positions. It involves understanding the people behind the roles: their hopes, burdens, gifts, limitations and experiences of church life.

Members may be reluctant to speak openly to a newly transferred minister. Some may need time to establish trust. Others may be watching to see whether the minister is genuinely interested in them or merely gathering information to advance a predetermined plan.

Trust cannot be demanded simply because a minister has been appointed. It grows through consistency, confidentiality, fairness and genuine concern. People are more likely to receive direction from a leader who has demonstrated a willingness to hear them.

This does not mean that a minister must become personally close to every member or agree with every account presented. Pastoral responsibility also requires appropriate boundaries, impartiality and careful judgment. The minister must be able to hear different perspectives without becoming captured by factions or drawn into unnecessary conflicts.

To know the sheep is to take their experiences seriously while remaining committed to the wider good of the congregation. It is to lead people as persons of dignity, not as instruments for implementing a minister’s ambitions.

Listening Is Not the Destination

Listening is essential, but it is not the whole of ministry. A minister who listens indefinitely without making decisions may leave a congregation uncertain about its direction. The purpose of listening is to inform faithful action.

After a period of attentive engagement, the minister should be able to identify priorities, communicate a vision and explain the steps required to move forward. Some initiatives may build on existing strengths; others may require reform. In either case, the congregation should understand not only what is being proposed but why it matters.

Where possible, members and church leaders should be involved in shaping the process. Participation does not mean that every decision must be made by popular vote. It means recognising that those who share responsibility for the Church’s life can contribute knowledge, experience and practical wisdom to its development.

Communication is particularly important when change affects established practices or relationships. Even a sound decision can be weakened by poor explanation, unnecessary confrontation or a failure to acknowledge the concerns of those affected.

A minister must therefore learn to move from listening to discernment, from discernment to decision, and from decision to responsible implementation. This movement allows listening to serve the Church’s mission rather than becoming an end in itself.

Practical Implications for Newly Transferred Ministers

The principles discussed above suggest several practical steps for ministers beginning a new pastoral assignment.

  1. Learn the history before proposing major changes. Seek to understand the congregation’s journey, previous leadership experiences, achievements and unresolved challenges. History does not determine the future, but it can help explain the present.
  2. Listen beyond the most visible voices. Engage church officers and influential members, but also create opportunities to hear younger members, quieter individuals and people whose contributions may be overlooked. A balanced understanding requires more than the perspective of a few prominent voices.
  3. Distinguish between urgent concerns and long-term priorities. Address serious risks and immediate responsibilities without delay. For other matters, allow sufficient time for consultation, reflection and planning.
  4. Recognise existing strengths. Do not assume that everything associated with the previous administration must be replaced. Identify what is working, acknowledge those who have contributed and build on practices that remain useful.
  5. Communicate the reasons for change. When change becomes necessary, explain its purpose, the concerns it seeks to address and the principles guiding the decision. Clear communication can reduce unnecessary suspicion and strengthen cooperation.
  6. Remain independent in judgment. Listen respectfully without allowing powerful individuals, competing factions or the desire for popularity to determine pastoral decisions. The minister is responsible for acting with integrity and fairness.
  7. Review progress and remain teachable. Leadership is not proved by the announcement of a plan but by its responsible implementation and its contribution to the Church’s life. Be willing to assess outcomes, acknowledge mistakes and make adjustments where necessary.

These practices cannot guarantee that every pastoral transition will be easy. They can, however, help ministers avoid preventable misunderstandings and approach their new responsibilities with greater wisdom.

A Challenge to the Contemporary Church

The responsibility for healthy pastoral transitions does not rest with ministers alone. The wider Church also has a role in preparing congregations and ministers for change.

Transfers are sometimes accompanied by high expectations but limited opportunities for orientation. A minister may be appointed to a congregation without adequate information about its history, pressing needs or existing commitments. Members, meanwhile, may expect immediate results without recognising the time required for a new minister to understand their circumstances.

Church authorities can help by providing appropriate transition information, clarifying expectations and encouraging incoming ministers to engage in meaningful consultation. Outgoing ministers can also contribute by communicating relevant information responsibly and helping to preserve continuity where possible.

Congregations, for their part, should allow new ministers room to learn while remaining open to thoughtful leadership. A transfer should not become an occasion for competing loyalties, the continuation of old conflicts or the testing of a minister’s authority.

Healthy transitions require patience from congregations, humility from ministers and wisdom from those responsible for appointments. When these responsibilities are taken seriously, pastoral transfer can become an opportunity for renewal rather than a source of unnecessary tension.

Conclusion

A newly transferred minister arrives with a calling to serve, teach, guide and lead. That calling should not be diminished by excessive caution or the fear of making difficult decisions. Yet neither should it become a justification for imposing changes before the congregation has been understood.

The first responsibility is to listen: to God through prayer and Scripture, to the congregation through attentive engagement, and to the realities of the ministry through careful observation and reflection. Listening creates the conditions for discernment, and discernment helps the minister identify when to preserve, when to reform and when to act decisively.

The aim is not to become a leader who pleases everyone. It is to become a leader whose decisions are informed by understanding, guided by conviction and carried out with integrity.

Pastoral leadership is not measured simply by how quickly a minister introduces an agenda. It is also measured by whether the minister understands the people, honours their dignity, recognises the gifts already present and helps the congregation move faithfully towards its calling.

Before the agenda, listen. Not because leadership must wait forever, but because leadership that listens is better prepared to lead well.
