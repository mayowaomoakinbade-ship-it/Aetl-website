Coexistence and Conviction: Navigating

Religious Exclusivism in a Pluralistic World

An Opinion Paper

The Rev’d. Olumayowa Alaba Akinbade

mayowaomoakinbade@gmail.com

Methodist Clergy and Advocate for Ethical and Transformational Leadership · AETL Opinion Series

Published by Advocacy for Ethical and Transformative Leadership (AETL)

“Promoting Ethical Leadership, Biblical Integrity and Transformative Ministry.”

Religious exclusivism holds that ultimate truth and salvation are uniquely found within one faith. For Christians, this conviction centers on Jesus Christ as the sole path to God (John 14:6). Exclusivism, however, does not justify hostility or coercion. Christian ethics emphasizes that truth must always be exercised with love. Paul instructs believers to “speak the truth in love” (Ephesians 4:15), reminding us that commitment to faith is inseparable from ethical responsibility.

In Nigeria, exclusivist beliefs pose an ethical challenge: how can Christians assert their faith without creating division? The principle “We may not be friends, but we don’t need to fight” provides guidance. Upholding conviction can coexist with humility, compassion, and respect, reflecting God’s love in practical ways.

Pluralism and Ethical Engagement

Nigeria is a pluralistic society. Cities often host close interaction between Christians and Muslims, while rural communities may feature overlapping religious and cultural identities. Pluralism can create friction, but it also offers opportunities for ethical engagement.

Engagement involves listening intentionally, respecting differing beliefs, and working together on common goals. Clergy, laity, and citizens can collaborate on initiatives addressing education, healthcare, poverty, and environmental challenges. Examples include:

Christian and Muslim communities jointly organizing literacy or health outreach programs.

Faith-based groups working together on community projects.

Dialogue forums that encourage understanding and prevent misunderstandings from escalating.

Such collaboration demonstrates that upholding one’s faith does not necessitate antagonism. The principle “We may not be friends, but we don’t need to fight” reminds us that pluralism can be an opportunity to live out justice, service, and moral integrity.

Faith Outreach as Responsible Witness

Exclusivist faith carries the responsibility of sharing its message. In a sensitive society like Nigeria, faith outreach must be ethical, relational, and guided by respect. The Great Commission (Matthew 28:19–20) calls for disciple-making, emphasizing authentic engagement and service rather than coercion.

Responsible faith outreach can take many forms:

Serving the community through tangible acts of support and charity.

Sharing personal experiences of faith to inspire rather than confront.

Engaging in dialogue that promotes mutual understanding.

Ethical faith outreach bridges conviction and coexistence. It demonstrates that being faithful to one’s beliefs can align with honoring the dignity and rights of others. The principle “We may not be friends, but we don’t need to fight” should guide all such interactions.

Hate Speech and Ethical Responsibility

In Nigeria’s diverse religious landscape, words carry immense power. Hate speech targeting other faiths whether in speeches, social media posts, or casual conversations can inflame tensions, deepen mistrust, and even provoke violence. Both clergy and laity have a responsibility to speak carefully and ethically.

Faithful living does not require silence about one’s beliefs, but it does demand wisdom, respect, and restraint. Christians are called to witness truth in love, not in condemnation or ridicule of others. The principle “We may not be friends, but we don’t need to fight” underscores that conviction and coexistence are compatible: one can maintain faith integrity while rejecting all forms of hate speech.

Practical steps to counter hate speech include:

Avoiding inflammatory language when discussing other faiths.

Correcting misinformation about other religious groups in a respectful manner.

Promoting dialogue and understanding instead of amplifying stereotypes.

Using sermons, community programs, and social platforms to model ethical speech and reconciliation.

By rejecting hate speech, believers reflect Christ’s character, safeguard social harmony, and enhance the credibility of their witness. Ethical speech is as important as ethical action in a pluralistic society.

Practical Applications in Clergy Work

Practical application of these principles is possible for everyone:

Community Service: Participating in initiatives that improve education, health, or livelihoods can unite people across religious lines.

Dialogue and Listening: Understanding neighbors of different faiths and clarifying misconceptions prevents conflict.

Ethical Witness: Acting with integrity, fairness, and compassion demonstrates faith more powerfully than words alone.

Education: Teaching children and young adults about respect for diversity, religious literacy, and conflict prevention builds a culture of peace.

Even small, everyday actions helping a neighbor, practicing honesty, or avoiding inflammatory speech can transform interactions into opportunities for coexistence. In all of these, the principle “We may not be friends, but we don’t need to fight” remains central.

Challenges and Theological Reflection

Living faithfully in Nigeria’s pluralistic society is not without tension. Exclusivist beliefs can be misinterpreted as arrogance or intolerance. Faith outreach can be misunderstood as intrusion. Historical, cultural, and political factors can exacerbate these tensions. These challenges require theological reflection, pastoral discernment, and ethical self-awareness. Clergy must cultivate both deep understanding of their own faith and sensitivity to the pluralistic contexts in which they minister.

Theologically, Christians are called to engage the world with both courage and humility, recognizing that ultimate judgment belongs to God (Romans 14:12). Ethical coexistence does not negate conviction; rather, it expresses it through love, patience, and respect.

Conclusion

In a pluralistic world, Christian exclusivism and peaceful coexistence need not be in conflict. The principle “We may not be friends, but we don’t need to fight” provides a theological and ethical framework for navigating diversity. By integrating responsible faith outreach, active service, and respectful dialogue, clergy, laity, and citizens can uphold their faith while fostering peace, understanding, and cooperation. Faithfulness to Christ, when combined with compassion and moral integrity, allows believers to witness effectively, live ethically, and contribute positively to a diverse world. In this way, pluralism becomes not a threat, but a context in which the love and truth of Christ can shine.
